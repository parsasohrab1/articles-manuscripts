Risk estimates are useful for decisions only if the stated probabilities agree with observed frequencies, yet many clinical tabular data sets are too small to set aside a separate calibration sample. We compare no calibration with Platt scaling, isotonic regression, beta calibration and temperature scaling for five classifiers on 15 public clinical and health-related data sets (155 to 2112 records, 32 to 606 events). Calibrators were fitted on inner cross-validated scores and evaluated by ten repetitions of stratified five-fold cross-validation; comparisons used data-set means, Wilcoxon tests with Holm correction and bootstrap intervals. On the Brier score, all four calibrators improved Gaussian naive Bayes (mean change -0.078 to -0.107, driven partly by a few severe cases) and gradient boosting (-0.0073 to -0.0096). No calibrator changed the Brier score significantly for logistic regression, nearest neighbours or random forest after correction. Isotonic regression raised the log loss of logistic regression on all 15 data sets (mean +0.042). Beta calibration and Platt scaling were the most frequent log-loss winners. The benefit of calibration therefore depends on the classifier and the metric, and it should not be applied to small clinical data by default.
